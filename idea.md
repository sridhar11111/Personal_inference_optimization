# On-Policy Layer Selection for LLM Depth Pruning — Full Research Plan

---

## Part A — The Research, Explained

### A.1 The problem

Depth pruning removes whole Transformer layers from an LLM to make it faster and smaller. The hard part is deciding **which** layers to remove.

Most methods score each layer with a **local** measurement taken on fixed text:

- **ShortGPT (Block Influence, BI):** how much a layer changes its hidden state (cosine similarity between input and output). Small change means "redundant".
- **KL-based selection (e.g. Minitron-style):** remove the layer, run the model on fixed text, and measure how much the next-token distribution changes.

Both measure damage on **reference text**: prefixes written by humans or by the full model. This is called **teacher forcing**, because the model is always fed the "correct" history.

### A.2 Why that may be the wrong question

At deployment, the pruned model is **not** fed correct history. It generates text autoregressively, and each token it produces becomes part of its own input.

If removing a layer causes small mistakes, those mistakes change the prefixes the model sees later. The model then operates on **states it created itself**, which may look nothing like the reference text it was scored on. This mismatch is known as **exposure bias**.

So a layer can look harmless under teacher forcing yet be harmful in real generation, because its removal pushes the model into unfamiliar states where it fails.

### A.3 The hypothesis

> **H1 (phenomenon):** Some layers have low damage on reference (teacher-forced) prefixes but high damage on the pruned model's own self-generated prefixes. Teacher-forced and on-policy rankings of layer importance therefore disagree.
>
> **H2 (method):** Selecting layers by on-policy damage produces better pruned models than selecting by local or teacher-forced scores, especially on long-form generation and reasoning.
>
> **H3 (robustness):** This advantage survives standard recovery fine-tuning, or reduces the recovery compute needed.

### A.4 Why this matters for Indic languages

Indic scripts typically split into **more tokens per word** than English. The same sentence therefore means a longer autoregressive trajectory, which gives self-induced drift more steps in which to build up.

**Prediction:** the gap between teacher-forced and on-policy rankings should be **larger for Indic languages than for English**. If true, it explains *why* English-calibrated local metrics transfer poorly to Indic generation. That is a distinctive angle for the paper.

### A.5 What is new and what is not

Be honest about this in the paper.

| Already exists | What we add |
|---|---|
| BI / cosine layer selection (ShortGPT) | — |
| One-step KL layer selection | — |
| Descriptions of autoregressive drift in pruned models | — |
| On-policy training to *recover* pruned models (ShortOPD) | — |
| — | **Using on-policy sensitivity, before any recovery, to decide which whole layers to remove** |
| — | **Measuring the exposure gap per layer, and across languages** |

> ⚠️ Verify ShortOPD, XMerge, TAP and every other cited paper yourself before relying on their claims. Several references came from ChatGPT outputs and are unverified.

## Part B — Definitions

### B.1 Notation

- **P**: the full model
- **Q_l**: the model with layer l removed (replaced by identity, so the residual stream passes straight through)
- **x**: a prompt
- **y_<k**: the generated prefix before position k
- **JS**: Jensen–Shannon divergence between two next-token distributions (symmetric and bounded)

### B.2 Teacher-forced damage

Prefixes are generated by the **full model**. Both models are scored on them.

```math
D^{TF}_l(H) = \frac{1}{H} \sum_{k=1}^{H} \mathbb{E}_{y_{<k} \sim P}\Big[ JS\big(P(\cdot \mid x, y_{<k}),\ Q_l(\cdot \mid x, y_{<k})\big) \Big]
```

**Meaning:** how much does removing layer l change predictions on "normal" states?

### B.3 On-policy damage

Prefixes are generated by the **pruned model itself**. Both models are scored on them.

```math
D^{OP}_l(H) = \frac{1}{H} \sum_{k=1}^{H} \mathbb{E}_{y_{<k} \sim Q_l}\Big[ JS\big(P(\cdot \mid x, y_{<k}),\ Q_l(\cdot \mid x, y_{<k})\big) \Big]
```

**Meaning:** how much does removing layer l change predictions on the states the pruned model actually visits?

The **same divergence (JS)** is used in both, so the only difference is **which states are visited**.

### B.4 Trajectory-level damage

Per-step JS has a blind spot. If the pruned model falls into a repetition loop or a wrong reasoning path, the full model may *agree* with it given that bad prefix, so JS stays low while the output is ruined.

To catch this, measure how unnatural the pruned model's trajectories look to the full model:

```math
T_l(H) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim Q_l}\Big[ -\frac{1}{H} \log P(y \mid x) \Big] - \mathbb{E}_{y \sim P}\Big[ -\frac{1}{H} \log P(y \mid x) \Big]
```

**Meaning:** the extra per-token negative log-likelihood, under the full model, of text written by the pruned model compared with text written by the full model. A high value means the pruned model has drifted somewhere the full model would not go.

### B.5 On-policy amplification ratio (diagnostic only)

```math
A_l(H) = \frac{D^{OP}_l(H)}{D^{TF}_l(H) + \epsilon}
```

- A ≈ 1: the layer causes similar damage on reference and self-generated states.
- A ≫ 1: the layer **looks safe on reference text but becomes damaging once the model visits its own states**.

> **Do not select layers by A.** The ratio is unstable when D^TF is small. Use it to *describe* layers; select by D^OP or T.

### B.6 Layer categories

| D^TF | A | Category | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | Low | **Safely redundant** | Remove it |
| Low | High | **Exposure-fragile** | Local metrics wrongly call it safe. These are the layers this paper is about |
| High | Low | Locally important | Everyone keeps it |
| High | High | Strongly important | Everyone keeps it |

## Part C — Experimental Setup

### C.1 Models

| Stage | Models | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Kill test and characterisation | Two ~1–3B models from **different families** (e.g. a Llama-3.2-3B-class model and a Qwen-2.5-3B-class model) | Cheap and fast. Two families show the effect is not one architecture's quirk |
| Main results | One or two ~7–8B models | Reviewers expect 7B+ for headline numbers |

Prefer models with reasonable Indic ability, otherwise the Indic results will be dominated by baseline weakness.

### C.2 Data

Use **separate** data for (a) computing layer scores (calibration) and (b) final evaluation. Never score layers on the test set.

| Purpose | English | Indic (your 3 languages) |
|---|---|---|
| Calibration prompts (scoring layers) | Mixed instructions, math word problems, short coding prompts | Instructions and questions in each language |
| Long-form generation eval | Summarization, open-ended answers | Summarization (e.g. XL-Sum covers several Indic languages), open generation |
| Reasoning eval | GSM8K | A translated or multilingual math benchmark covering your languages (check language coverage first) |
| Code eval | HumanEval / MBPP | — |
| Static sanity checks | MMLU, ARC | An Indic classification/QA benchmark |
| Translation (optional) | — | FLORES (EN to Indic) |

> Check that each benchmark actually covers your three chosen languages before committing. Coverage varies by dataset.

### C.3 Fixed choices (keep identical across all methods)

- **Removal operator:** identity skip for every method. No reconstruction or merging, unless *every* method gets the same one. Otherwise reviewers will say the gain came from reconstruction, not selection.
- **Decoding for scoring:** sampling at temperature 1.0 to estimate distributions, plus a greedy variant that matches how you evaluate.
- **Seeds:** at least 3 seeds for every on-policy measurement.
- **Calibration size:** start with roughly 200–300 prompts per language × 4 rollouts each.

## Part D — The Plan, Phase by Phase

Each phase lists **what to do**, **what to observe**, and **what it means**.

---

### Phase 0 — Infrastructure and sanity checks (≈ 3–5 days)

**What to do**

1. Implement layer removal as an identity skip with a single flag per layer.
2. Implement ShortGPT BI and reproduce its layer ranking on one model.
3. Implement one-step KL selection.
4. Implement the rollout loop: generate from Q_l, then score both P and Q_l on that prefix. Cache P's distributions to save compute.
5. Measure the wall-clock cost of scoring one layer.

**What to observe**

- The BI ranking looks like published results (early and late layers important, middle layers more redundant).
- Removing no layers gives JS = 0 exactly (a bug check).
- The cost per layer, which you can extrapolate to the full experiment.

**What it means**

- If BI doesn't reproduce, your pipeline has a bug. Fix it before anything else, because every later comparison depends on it.
- The cost number tells you whether full greedy selection on 7B is feasible, or whether you need the cheaper variants in Phase 3.

---

### Phase 1 — The kill test (≈ 1 week)

> This phase decides whether the project continues. Do not skip ahead.

**What to do**

For each small model, each language (English + 3 Indic), and every layer l:

1. Compute D^TF_l(H) and D^OP_l(H) at H = 64.
2. Compute T_l(H).
3. Rank the layers by each score.

**What to observe**

1. **Spearman correlation** between the D^TF and D^OP rankings.
2. **Overlap of the safest-k layers**: of the k layers each method would remove first (e.g. k = 4 and 8), how many are shared? Measure this as Jaccard overlap. It matters more than full-ranking correlation, because only the layers you actually remove count.
3. A **scatter plot** of D^TF (x-axis) against D^OP (y-axis), one point per layer.
4. Whether the correlation is **lower for Indic languages than for English**.

**What it means**

| Observation | Interpretation | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Spearman ≥ 0.9 **and** safest-k overlap ≈ 100% | Teacher-forced scoring already picks the same layers. No exposure gap | Go to the fallbacks in Part E (check subgroups first) |
| Spearman 0.7–0.9, partial overlap | A weak effect. It may matter only for some tasks, languages or depths | Continue, but focus Phase 2 on finding *where* it is strong |
| Spearman < 0.7 **or** safest-k overlap ≤ 50% | **The effect is real**: the two methods would remove different layers | Continue with full confidence |
| Clear off-diagonal points in the scatter (low D^TF, high D^OP) | Exposure-fragile layers exist | These layers are the paper's central example. Study them in Phase 2 |
| Gap larger for Indic than English | Supports the token-length explanation | This becomes a headline finding |

---

### Phase 2 — Characterise the phenomenon (≈ 2 weeks)

**What to do**

1. **Horizon sweep:** compute rankings at H = 16, 64, 128, 256.
2. **Task split:** separate calibration prompts into short QA, math reasoning, code and long-form generation, then compute rankings for each.
3. **Stability:** repeat across 3 seeds and 2 decoding settings (sampling and greedy).
4. **Failure analysis for exposure-fragile layers:** for the layers with high A, inspect the rollouts. Record the first token where P and Q_l diverge, the repetition/degeneration rate, and the task correctness of the rollouts.
5. **Token-length check:** for each language, record tokens per word and plot it against the size of the exposure gap.

**What to observe**

- How the D^TF–D^OP correlation changes as H grows.
- Which tasks show the largest gap.
- Whether rankings are stable across seeds (do the confidence intervals of neighbouring layers overlap?).
- *What kind* of failure exposure-fragile layers cause: repetition, wrong reasoning, language switching (common in Indic generation), or early termination.
- Whether D^OP and T agree.

**What it means**

| Observation | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Correlation drops as H grows, then levels off | Drift accumulates over the trajectory. The plateau point tells you the cheapest horizon worth using |
| Gap large for reasoning and long-form, small for short QA | Matches the theory: exposure bias needs long self-generated sequences. It also explains why MMLU-style benchmarks hide the problem |
| Rankings unstable across seeds | D^OP is too noisy at this sample size. Increase prompts/rollouts before Phase 3, or the method will not be reproducible |
| D^OP low but T high for some layers | The per-step blind spot is real (degeneration the full model "agrees" with). Use T, or a combination, for selection |
| Exposure-fragile layers cause language switching in Indic | A concrete, language-specific failure mode. Strong qualitative material for the paper |
| Gap grows with tokens per word | Direct evidence for the Indic hypothesis in A.4 |

### Phase 3 — The selection method (≈ 2 weeks)

**What to do**

1. **Greedy on-policy selection:**

```text
Start with the full model
        ↓
Score every remaining layer (D^OP or T, chosen from Phase 2)
        ↓
Remove the safest layer
        ↓
Re-score the remaining layers using rollouts from the *current* pruned model
        ↓
Repeat until the target depth is reached
```

   Re-scoring matters because removing one layer can change whether its neighbours are still safe.

2. **Cheaper variants** (needed for 7B+):
   - Score once, then re-score only the top-k candidates at each step.
   - Use fewer prompts with the shortest horizon that worked in Phase 2.

3. **Baselines** at **equal depth** (e.g. 32 → 28 and 32 → 24 layers), all with the same identity-skip operator:
   - ShortGPT BI
   - One-step KL
   - **Teacher-forced multi-step KL/JS (D^TF)**, the strongest and most important baseline
   - Random layer selection (lower bound)

**What to observe**

- Which layers each method removes.
- Quality of each pruned model on the Phase-5 evaluation suite (run a small version here).
- Selection cost (GPU-hours) for each method.

**What it means**

| Observation | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| On-policy beats D^TF on generation/reasoning but ties on MMLU | Exactly the predicted pattern: the gain appears where exposure bias acts |
| On-policy beats BI but **not** D^TF | The gain comes from using KL instead of cosine, not from being on-policy. The core claim fails, so go to the fallbacks |
| The cheap variant matches full greedy | The method is practical at scale, which strengthens the paper |
| Gains grow with pruning ratio | Errors from multiple removed layers interact. Greedy on-policy re-scoring captures this |

---

### Phase 4 — The recovery test (≈ 2 weeks)

> Reviewers will ask: *"Why not select with BI and then run on-policy recovery training?"* This phase answers that.

**What to do**

Run a 2×2 experiment at one target depth:

| | No recovery | Same recovery (fixed budget) |
|---|---|---|
| **BI selection** | ✓ | ✓ |
| **On-policy selection** | ✓ | ✓ |

Also track quality **during** recovery (evaluate every N steps) to get recovery curves.

Use one standard recovery method for both rows: LoRA fine-tuning or distillation from the full model, with the same data, steps and hyperparameters.

**What to observe**

- The final quality gap after recovery.
- How many recovery steps each method needs to reach a fixed quality level.

**What it means**

| Observation | Interpretation | Paper framing |
|---|---|---|
| On-policy still wins after recovery | Selection matters even with training | The strongest outcome: main-conference claim |
| Gap closes after recovery, but on-policy gets there faster | Selection saves recovery compute | "Same quality with X% less recovery compute" |
| Gap closes and recovery speed is equal | Selection does not matter once you train | Frame for the **training-free** setting, or report it as a finding (see Part E) |

---

### Phase 5 — Full evaluation (≈ 2 weeks)

**What to do**

Evaluate every pruned model from Phases 3–4 on:

- Static: MMLU, ARC, and an Indic QA/classification set
- Perplexity (English and each Indic language)
- Reasoning: GSM8K and a multilingual math set
- Code: HumanEval/MBPP
- Long-form: summarization in English and Indic languages
- Generation health: repetition rate, degeneration rate, and **language-consistency rate** (does the output stay in the target language?)

Report results on the 7B-class model(s) as headline numbers.

**What to observe**

- Where the gains appear (static vs. generative tasks).
- English vs. Indic gains.
- Speedup (tokens/sec) at each depth. It should be the same for all methods at equal depth; confirm this.

**What it means**

- Gains concentrated on generative tasks confirm that **static benchmarks hide pruning damage**. This is a secondary contribution about evaluation practice.
- Larger Indic gains support the claim that English-calibrated pruning transfers poorly to Indic generation.

---

### Phase 6 (optional) — A cheap proxy (≈ 1–2 weeks)

**What to do**

Find a signal that predicts D^OP **without rollouts**, for example teacher-forced JS at late positions only, hidden-state norm growth, or attention entropy.

**What to observe**

The Spearman correlation between each proxy and D^OP across layers.

**What it means**

A good proxy turns the method from accurate but expensive into **accurate and cheap**. That is valuable if Phase 3 gains are real but modest, and it is a strong contribution on its own.

## Part E — If Results Don't Go Our Way

| Failure | First check | Fallback paper |
|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1: rankings agree everywhere** | Is the gap present for long generation, high pruning ratios (multi-layer greedy), or Indic only? | (a) A narrower paper on the subgroup where it holds; (b) a careful negative result: "teacher-forced KL is sufficient for layer selection" (workshop / Findings); (c) return to the **original Indic pruning paper**: English vs. Indic layer redundancy, using the per-layer data already collected |
| **Phase 3: on-policy beats BI but not D^TF** | Does T (trajectory metric) beat D^TF where D^OP doesn't? | Reframe as "multi-step KL beats cosine for Indic pruning". Modest, but publishable with the Indic angle |
| **Phase 4: recovery erases the gain** | Is there a recovery-compute saving? | (a) Training-free compression framing; (b) a compute-savings paper; (c) the finding "selection matters little once you do on-policy recovery" |
| **Gains real but small** | Can a cheap proxy match them? | An efficiency paper built on Phase 6 |

**Built-in protection:** because Phase 1 already uses two model families, several task types and four languages, even a negative result is a characterised finding, not a single failed run.

## Part G — Paper Outline (if all goes well)

1. **Introduction:** depth-pruning selection is scored on reference text, but deployment is autoregressive. We show these disagree.
2. **Related work:** layer pruning (BI, KL), autoregressive drift in pruned models, on-policy recovery, multilingual/Indic compression.
3. **The exposure gap:** definitions (D^TF, D^OP, T, A), the scatter plot, and the layer categories.
4. **Characterisation:** effects of horizon, task and language; failure modes; the token-length link.
5. **Method:** greedy on-policy selection and its cheap variant.
6. **Experiments:** equal-depth comparison, the recovery 2×2, and the full evaluation.
7. **Analysis:** why static benchmarks hide the damage; English vs. Indic.
8. **Limitations:** compute cost, decoding dependence, and that the scores measure sensitivity to self-induced states, not causal error chains.

## Part H — Rules That Keep the Paper Defensible

1. Use the **same removal operator** for every method.
2. Use the **same divergence (JS)** for teacher-forced and on-policy scores.
3. Keep **calibration and evaluation data separate**.
4. Make **teacher-forced multi-step JS the main baseline**, not BI.
5. Report **confidence intervals** and **selection cost**.
6. Use the language of **"exposure sensitivity"**, never "error at token 1 caused later errors".
7. **Verify every citation** yourself.
